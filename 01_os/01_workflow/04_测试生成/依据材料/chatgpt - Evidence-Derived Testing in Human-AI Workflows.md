## Are existing test-generation methods specification-driven

**What the literature says.** In software, the center of gravity is where you feared it would be. QuickCheck’s original formulation says the programmer formulates **properties of programs**, and later QuickCheck literature is explicit that it tests software **against a formal specification** using developer-specified properties. Dan North’s original BDD essay says that a story’s behavior is its **acceptance criteria**, captured as Given/When/Then scenarios, and Cucumber’s official documentation says it reads **executable specifications** and checks that software does what those specifications say. Requirements-based and contract-based generation are even more overtly specification-driven. citeturn36search13turn36search2turn24view2turn37search0turn37search7turn33search4

There are important exceptions, but they do not rescue the independence problem. Search-based and structural test generation often start from **source code and coverage goals**, not from specs; coverage-oriented generation is a large literature of its own. Mutation testing also does not start from a formal spec: it perturbs code and asks whether the current tests kill the mutants. However, both families usually lack an independent acceptance oracle. They are excellent for exploration, regression hardening, and fault exposure, but they do not solve your core problem of deriving a criterion from an external third party. citeturn35search0turn33search13turn22search4turn22search23

A closer partial exception is **metamorphic testing**. It was created for the oracle problem: when you cannot easily know the exact correct output, you can still test whether necessary relations hold across related inputs and outputs. That is a genuine departure from example-based expected outputs. But metamorphic testing still depends on **metamorphic relations** that encode necessary properties of the target function. In other words, it replaces a full oracle with a **partial specification**. It is not “raw evidence to test” in your sense; it is “a weaker, still-specifying relation to test.” citeturn20search3turn20search18turn20search7

**What I infer.** So the precise answer to your first question is: **no, not all existing test generation is from specifications**, because some of it is generated from code structure, coverage objectives, constraints, or mutant behavior. **But** nearly all the methods that generate a meaningful **acceptance oracle** are still driven by some internally stated behavioral description: a property, a requirement, a contract, an acceptance criterion, or a model. On the independence dimension you care about, your criticism still lands. citeturn35search0turn36search2turn37search0turn22search4

The strongest positive precedents for evidence-first testing come from science rather than software. *Daubert* explicitly quoted the Popperian idea that scientific methodology proceeds by generating hypotheses and trying to test or falsify them. Mayo’s severe-testing program sharpens that into a criterion: a claim is well-supported only if it has passed a test that **would probably have found flaws if the claim were false**. Preregistration literature makes the same separation operational by distinguishing **confirmatory** analysis from exploratory analysis and requiring key tests and outcomes to be **prespecified**. That is not identical to “derive tests directly from evidence,” but it is the most mature existing family of practices aimed at preventing the same self-sealing loop you are trying to avoid. citeturn23view3turn31search6turn31search0turn23view6turn39search0turn39search3

## How descriptive evidence becomes prescriptive tests

**What the literature says.** The literature does not support a general rule that descriptive evidence mechanically turns into normative tests without any interpretive step. Modern validity theory is explicit that what is being justified is not “the test in itself,” but the **interpretation and use** of the result. Kane’s argument-based framework says validation starts by making explicit which interpretation/use is intended, and then collecting the evidence needed to support that interpretation. Assurance-case literature says essentially the same thing in another vocabulary: evidence does not support a claim by magic; it supports a claim **through an argument or warrant**. citeturn18search3turn25search12turn25search18turn23view8turn19search17

That means the bridge from “the world is like this” to “the system should satisfy this test” is usually a **warrant selection step**. In plain terms, someone must state: given this evidence, which claim about B is justified, which observable would discriminate success from failure, and what error/uncertainty budget is acceptable? Metrology is unusually crisp here. NIST says traceability is a property of a **measurement result**, not of an instrument; supporting a traceability claim requires a clearly defined property, a documented process, a stated result with uncertainty, and an unbroken documented chain to the reference. That is exactly the kind of bridge your workflow needs between evidence and test. citeturn32view0

**What I infer.** The conversion is therefore not “descriptive evidence becomes normative test” in one jump. It is closer to this chain: **evidence → atomic observation or constraint → claim/construct to be checked → observable test method → threshold / failure condition**. The unavoidable human judgment is not arbitrary taste; it is the choice of **warrant**. Specifically: which property of the world matters here, which operationalization measures it, which uncertainty is tolerable, and whether one failing item falsifies the claim or merely lowers confidence. That judgment can be made explicit, reviewed, versioned, and audited, but I did not find a serious literature claiming it can be eliminated in the general case. citeturn18search3turn23view8turn32view0turn31search6

This is also where your “evidence, not B” rule becomes strongest. If B is allowed to choose its own warrant, it can select observables that are easiest for itself to satisfy. Science’s “risky test” and severe-testing traditions are useful here because they privilege observations that would genuinely have had a good chance of exposing error. The right human judgment is therefore not just “what can we test,” but “what observation would honestly hurt B if B were wrong.” citeturn23view3turn31search6turn31search0

## How to make evidence-to-test traceability structural rather than aspirational

**What the literature says.** Software traceability research already distinguishes **pre-requirements-specification traceability** from later traceability. Pre-RS traceability links requirements to their origins, such as interviews, meeting notes, and legacy artifacts. The 2024 systematic review found that this area is under-researched relative to later-stage traceability, that the data are often unstructured and dynamic, that obsolete links are a known problem, and that perceived effort is often too high. Separate empirical work reports that traceability in practice is still mainly manual, that tool support is limited, and that cost and weak collaboration keep traceability immature. An industrial experience report showed links degrading over time and noted that missing simple requirement–test links hindered follow-on work. citeturn26view1turn27search0turn27search3turn11search13

At the same time, the provenance and assurance-case literatures point toward a structural answer. W3C PROV models provenance in terms of **entities, activities, and agents** and allows constraints and validators over derivation relations. Assurance cases and GSN explicitly capture **claims, arguments, and evidence**, and one of their explicit motivations is preserving traceability between claims and substantiating evidence. Metrology adds a useful discipline: a valid traceability claim needs a clearly defined property, documented method, uncertainty statement, reference chain, and assigned responsibility; traceability alone still does not guarantee fitness for purpose. citeturn23view1turn23view2turn19search12turn19search11turn32view0

**What I infer.** A structural implementation is very plausible. Treat each **test** as a provenance entity that must be `wasGeneratedBy` a **derivation activity** that `used` one or more **evidence entities**, and that `wasAssociatedWith` a responsible human or agent. Store, for every test, at least: evidence IDs, extracted atomic proposition, operationalization/warrant, measurement method, threshold, uncertainty/error assumptions, and last verification time. Then make your schema reject any test case that lacks those fields, just as you already reject unsupported claims elsewhere. That is not a mere process norm; it is enforceable data shape. This step is an inference from PROV, assurance-case, and metrological-traceability sources, not something I found prepackaged under one library name. citeturn23view1turn23view2turn23view8turn32view0

The failure cases are real and should not be waved away. The pre-RS review says perceived burden is high, obsolete links are common, benefits are often unclear, and precise automated extraction is still limited. The IR-based trace-recovery mapping study is blunt that industrial evidence is weak and many evaluations are on small datasets. So “make every test traceable to evidence” is feasible as a structure, but there is no evidence that ordinary teams get this for free just by adopting an RTM. citeturn26view1turn26view2turn27search0turn27search3

## What makes a test good enough to count as acceptance evidence

**What the literature says.** *Daubert* and Rule 702 are useful because they are explicit gatekeeping frameworks. The court’s factors include whether the theory or technique can be and has been tested, whether it has been peer reviewed/published, its known or potential error rate, the existence of standards controlling its operation, and whether it has widespread acceptance. The inquiry is flexible and focuses on **principles and methodology**, not merely conclusions. Rule 702, especially after the 2023 amendment, also stresses that admissibility requires the proponent to show it is **more likely than not** that the method and its application satisfy the rule; several courts had been incorrectly treating basis and application issues as mere “weight.” In Canada, *Mohan* asks for relevance, necessity, absence of exclusionary rule, and a properly qualified expert. citeturn23view3turn23view5turn6search1

This is a **valid analogy in one limited sense**: if you want to know whether a candidate test procedure is credible enough to be admitted as evidence in an acceptance decision, Daubert-like factors are helpful. A test that has no known operating conditions, no estimated false-positive/false-negative behavior, no controlled procedure, and no community scrutiny is weak acceptance evidence by any sensible standard. This is exactly how Daubert-style reasoning is used in adjacent computational domains such as digital forensics software validation. citeturn23view3turn17search2turn17search4

But it is also an **incomplete and potentially misleading analogy** if used alone. Daubert is about admissibility of expert methods in adjudication. Psychometric validity is closer to your real question because it asks whether **evidence and theory support the interpretation and use** of the result for the specific decision at hand. NIST makes an analogous point in measurement science: traceability does not guarantee that uncertainty is adequate for purpose, nor that there are no mistakes. So the central issue for your workflow is not just “is the test method respectable,” but “does this test actually measure the property whose satisfaction we want to accept?” Daubert is a reliability floor; validity and fitness-for-purpose are the better standards for the acceptance question. citeturn25search12turn25search18turn32view0

There are also important criticisms of the legal standard itself. Commentators have argued that judges are often poor scientific gatekeepers, that peer review and general acceptance are only proxies for quality, and that Daubert’s flexibility can produce inconsistency or unfair exclusion/admission. The Rule 702 amendment itself was driven by widespread judicial misapplication of what should count as admissibility rather than mere weight. I did **not** find a mature literature directly applying a full Daubert-style framework to ordinary software acceptance tests in general; the closest hits were mostly in digital-forensics admissibility, not software-workflow design. citeturn7search8turn7search14turn7search22turn23view5turn17search2turn17search18

## What “validating B before implementation” can really mean

**What the literature says.** Formal methods genuinely can provide information before implementation, but the information is specific. Model checking was created as an automatic way to verify whether a **model** satisfies formally stated properties. Alloy’s entire pitch is to model software design independently of, and even before, implementation, and to automatically generate examples and counterexamples. NASA-style early-validation case studies found that formal methods could validate requirements early in the lifecycle at reasonable cost, using natural-language “shall” statements, formal models, model checking, theorem proving, and simulation. citeturn14search11turn14search1turn14search12turn21search0turn21search12turn21search9

So before implementation, tests can evaluate at least four things: whether B is **internally consistent**, whether a formalized version of B is **satisfiable / realizable**, whether specified bad states are **reachable or unreachable**, and whether there exist **counterexamples** under bounded or abstracted assumptions. That is real information. It is stronger than a thought experiment and weaker than field validation. citeturn14search4turn14search12turn14search11

**What I infer.** The right meaning of “validate feasibility before implementation” is therefore narrow but important: you can often show that **there exists at least one coherent model/design that satisfies the evidence-derived tests**, or conversely show that your proposed B is inconsistent, underspecified, or impossible under the formalized assumptions. That is valuable because it can kill bad Bs early. It does **not** show that a later implementation will satisfy those properties in production, nor that the formal model captured the world correctly. citeturn21search0turn14search4turn14search12

The limits matter. Model checking suffers from the classic **state explosion problem**. Alloy’s analysis is bounded and leans on the **small scope hypothesis**, which is useful but incomplete. Formal-methods practice is also expensive in expertise and modeling effort. So a pre-implementation “pass” is informative in the sense of ruling out contradictions and finding some kinds of impossibility or counterexample early; it is not a general warranty that B is implementable in the messy world you will eventually face. citeturn15search3turn15search12turn15search15turn15search9

## How FActScore-like methods can and cannot be used on B

**What the literature says.** FActScore decomposes a long-form output into **atomic facts** and computes the percentage of those facts that are supported by a reliable knowledge source. That makes it a natural candidate for quantifying “how much of B is supported by external evidence.” SAFE extends the idea by decomposing long-form responses into individual facts, verifying them via search, and balancing supported-fact precision with a recall-like notion through an extended F1-style metric. citeturn8search6turn9search9turn29view0

This can be adapted to your workflow, but only under strong preconditions. B must be decomposable into **atomic, externally checkable claims**; the evidence source must be sufficiently complete and time-aligned; you need a protocol for extraction, retrieval, adjudication, and aggregation; and you need provenance to the evidence passages used in each verdict. The multilingual FActScore analysis is especially relevant here because it shows the score is highly sensitive to the **quality and coverage of the knowledge source** and to failures in claim extraction and translation. citeturn28view0turn8search7turn29view0

The known failure modes are exactly the ones you would expect your workflow to care about. The original FActScore work itself says its evaluation is mainly on **people biographies using Wikipedia**, and flags generalization as future work. The multilingual analysis finds that no evaluator is consistently reliable across languages, that extraction quality drops in lower-resource languages, and that Wikipedia’s limited coverage can depress the estimated score. SAFE improves scalability, but its own headline result still means substantial residual error: 72% agreement with crowd annotators and a win rate of 76% on a 100-case disagreement sample. OpenFActScore also points out a conceptual issue: “extrinsic hallucinations” can add information not grounded in the prompt yet still be factually correct, which complicates any simple support metric. citeturn9search0turn28view0turn29view0turn28view1

**What I infer.** So yes, a FActScore-like layer can quantify **claim-support density** for B, but it is best understood as a **support-audit metric**, not as your acceptance test generator itself. It tells you, roughly, “of the claims B makes, what fraction are externally supported under this retrieval-and-adjudication pipeline?” It does **not** by itself tell you whether B omitted an essential claim, whether the supported claims measure the right construct, or whether a supported proposition has been turned into a falsifiable acceptance test. In your terms: FactScore can help score the evidence behind B’s claims, but a second step is still needed to convert claim support into **honest failure conditions**. citeturn8search6turn29view0turn28view0turn25search12
