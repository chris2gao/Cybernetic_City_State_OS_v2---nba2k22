## Scope, added domains, and overall answer

I treated all five domains you named as relevant, and I did **not** find a strong reason to discard any of them. In practice, however, the research pushed two additional domains to the foreground because they match your implementation constraint unusually well. The first is **assurance cases** from safety-critical engineering, especially Goal Structuring Notation and the OMG Structured Assurance Case Metamodel, because that field is explicitly about organizing **claims, arguments, and evidence** into auditable structures. The second is **probabilistic graphical models and provenance verification**, because Bayes-network work exists specifically as an extension or critique of ACH, and modern provenance-verification work exists specifically to test whether a structured claim is actually supported by the cited source text. Those added domains are not replacements for your five; they are practical bridges between them. citeturn34search0turn34search1turn30view4turn30view5turn35view0

The short answer to your five questions is this. **ACH can be semi-automated, but mostly on the bookkeeping, propagation, sorting, and comparative-analysis side, not on the framing side.** ACH’s real center of gravity is still evaluative, not generative. The literature repeatedly distinguishes **hypothesis generation** from **hypothesis evaluation**, and the strongest concrete companion method I found for generation in an ACH-adjacent workflow is **morphological analysis**, including intelligence-specific implementations that link evidence to morphological dimensions and then propagate support semi-automatically to large hypothesis sets. For “every claim must hang on evidence,” the most implementation-ready answer is not pure Toulmin theory by itself, but a combination of **assurance-case notation or a machine-readable argument graph**, plus **provenance standards**, plus **graph validation constraints**. GRADE is also useful, but not because it gives a simple formula from “evidence strength” to “recommendation strength”; rather, it gives a structured decision record that forces panels to expose the non-evidential criteria that also matter. Finally, there are several operational candidate-generation methods from design and intelligence practice, and they are procedural enough to be wired into a workflow rather than left as “be creative.” citeturn11search14turn29view0turn29view3turn40view0turn41view0turn14search0turn14search1turn18view0turn18view2turn19view5

My strongest implementation-level conclusion is an **inference**, not a direct quote from any one source: if your workflow is “structured requirement A + verified external evidence → modeled result B where every claim in B cites evidence,” the literature does **not** point to one monolithic method. It points to a **composed pipeline**: use a generation method to produce candidate Bs or candidate subclaims, use ACH-like or Bayesian comparative methods to prune and rank them, and store the surviving structure in a machine-checkable claim–evidence graph with provenance and validation rules. ## ACH automation and what still resists automation

For your first question, the primary ACH workflow is still the one set out by Heuer and then reproduced in later empirical work: identify possible hypotheses, list significant evidence and arguments, build a matrix with evidence down the side and hypotheses across the top, mark each cell as consistent, inconsistent, or not applicable, refine the matrix by removing non-diagnostic items and reconsidering hypotheses, then draw tentative conclusions by trying to **disprove** hypotheses rather than prove them. In the later empirical paper by Dhami, Belton, and Mandel, Step 5 is described concretely as summing the **inconsistent ratings** for each hypothesis to obtain an “inconsistency score,” then ranking hypotheses so that the **lowest inconsistency score** is tentatively most likely. Heuer’s original text also stresses deleting evidence and arguments that have no diagnostic value and rewording or combining hypotheses when necessary. citeturn5view1turn5view2turn7view1

That means the mechanical core of ACH is automatable at a fairly obvious level. A tool can store the matrix, track cell labels, compute inconsistency counts, sort evidence by diagnosticity, sort by source type, separate older from newer evidence, and re-run the tally after cell edits. Heuer’s later software write-up says PARC-developed ACH software estimated a rough probability for each hypothesis from analyst-entered consistency judgments and allowed evidence to be sorted in analytically useful ways, including by diagnosticity and by source type. PARC’s technical description also shows that tool builders tried at least two scoring backends: plain **inconsistency counting** and a **normalized** multiplicative algorithm that behaves more like a probability model. citeturn9search0turn30view2

There were also explicit attempts to move beyond a small hand-built matrix. The Swedish FOI MHMA work is especially relevant to your workflow because it combines **hypothesis generation** and **evidence management**. Its key move is to link evidence not to every full hypothesis separately, but to elements of a **morphological chart**, so that support or contradiction propagates across all hypotheses containing that condition. The authors describe this as “semi-automatic” assessment, explicitly because direct ACH over all generated hypotheses becomes infeasible when the hypothesis set grows large. In other words, once the hypothesis space is combinatorial, the first useful automation is not “let the model think like an analyst,” but “let the system propagate local evidence judgments through a structured hypothesis space.” citeturn29view0turn29view1turn29view2turn29view3

So what still has to stay human-heavy? The best direct evidence is negative. Dhami, Belton, and Mandel say ACH relies on the **judgment skills of the analyst** and on the analyst’s **subjective input** and interpretation of outputs; in their experiment, most trained analysts deviated from one or more prescribed ACH steps, especially the evidence-integration step. The same study found no significant improvement in accuracy versus an untrained group, even though ACH changed the form of reasoning and increased tied-rank conclusions. Earlier work by Lehner and colleagues found confirmation-bias reduction only for participants **without** intelligence-analysis experience, and not for experienced analysts. Later work outside intelligence, in legal decision-making, likewise reported that ACH did not significantly reduce confirmation bias. Recent reviews in intelligence studies now say ACH lacks strong empirical support and may sometimes fail to improve judgment quality. citeturn7view0turn7view1turn31search8turn33search0turn33search4turn6search3turn6search8turn33search6

The strongest critique of ACH automation is therefore not “software cannot add up the matrix.” It is that **the hard part is upstream of the tally**. Humans still have to decide whether the hypothesis set is well framed, whether evidence items are independent, whether deception or rumor structure is present, whether a piece of evidence is truly diagnostic, and whether the hypotheses are sufficiently exhaustive and mutually exclusive. PARC’s ACH0 technical note is explicit that the inconsistency-counting algorithm does **not** provide a probabilistic basis for comparing hypotheses, that both algorithms assume evidence independence, and that the normalized algorithm also assumes hypotheses are mutually exclusive and exhaustive. That is a direct warning against over-trusting automatic scores. citeturn30view2

My implementation inference from the retrieved material is therefore narrow but strong. **Give tools and models the repetitive structure work**: matrix maintenance, evidence extraction candidates, cell pre-label suggestions, inconsistency aggregation, diagnosticity sorting, provenance capture, and sensitivity reruns. **Keep humans in charge of problem formulation and critical evidential judgments**: hypothesis framing, variable selection, evidence dependency, deception/counter-evidence, and final interpretive sign-off. That division is more consistent with the literature than “full ACH automation.” citeturn7view0turn9search0turn29view3turn30view2turn30view5

## Where candidate hypotheses come from when ACH itself is not enough

For your second question, the literature supports a precise answer: **ACH includes a hypothesis-listing step, but its operational heart is evaluation, not generation**. Heuer’s Step 1 says to identify possible hypotheses and use analysts with different perspectives to brainstorm possibilities, and later teaching material explicitly distinguishes **hypothesis generation** from **hypothesis evaluation**. But the computational and empirical literature around ACH repeatedly treats the hypotheses as already given and then focuses on matrix evaluation. The FOI follow-on paper says that in many ACH examples “the hypotheses are even given in the task,” which is exactly the generation gap you are worried about. citeturn11search10turn11search14turn29view3

The most concrete companion method I found is **morphological analysis**. In the FOI MHMA work, morphological analysis is used “more or less in its standard form to generate the hypotheses,” while ACH ideas are used to connect evidence to those hypotheses. The same paper says morphological analysis systematically generates plausible hypotheses or future developments by defining key parameters and their values, then exploring combinations; it also warns that standard ACH cannot be run directly over all generated hypotheses because the set becomes too large. Their answer is not to abandon generation, but to push evidence down to the parameter/value level, where evidence links become reusable across many candidate hypotheses. That is a very direct response to your “where do candidate hypotheses come from?” question. citeturn29view0turn29view1turn29view3

Ritchey’s general morphological analysis provides the generic method behind that move. It structures a conceptual problem space as a **morphospace**, then uses **cross-consistency assessment** to remove mutually incompatible value pairs and shrink the space to internally consistent configurations. Ritchey explicitly describes GMA as a method for structuring a conceptual problem space and synthesizing a solution space, and explains that cross-consistency assessment weeds out combinations that are logically, empirically, or normatively inconsistent. That makes GMA a true **candidate generator with pruning**, not just a brainstorming aid. citeturn12search8turn27view3turn27view4

A second family of companion methods comes from **systematic design**. Pahl and Beitz’s process starts with task clarification and a requirements list, then moves to conceptual design by abstracting the essential problems, building function structures, searching for working principles, and combining them into working structures. They explicitly recommend morphological matrices for combining solution elements, while warning that the main problems are physical/geometric compatibility and the need to select technically and economically favorable combinations from a large combinatorial field. That is not intelligence jargon, but structurally it is the same problem you described for generating a modeled “what the requirement would look like if realized.” citeturn27view0turn27view2

A third family is **C-K theory**, which is more abstract but still operational enough to matter. Hatchuel and colleagues define design as co-expansion of a **concept space** and a **knowledge space**, with four operators linking them. The practical implication is that generation is not treated as a one-shot “invent hypotheses” step, but as an iterative expansion in which new concepts trigger new knowledge searches and new knowledge enables new concepts. That is useful if your B is not merely one hypothesis but a structured outcome model that may need multiple rounds of refinement against the evidence base. citeturn27view5turn12search10

A fourth family is **TRIZ/ARIZ**, which treats generation as contradiction resolution. Altshuller’s ARIZ instructions begin with formulating a mini-problem and defining conflicting elements, and later TRIZ materials describe the contradiction matrix as mapping an improving parameter and a worsening parameter to candidate inventive principles. However, the same literature is candid that ARIZ is difficult to follow and requires more intellectual effort, and that the inventive principles do **not** supply ready-made solutions; they give directions for thought. That makes TRIZ more useful as a structured generator of candidate moves than as a complete end-to-end workflow. citeturn28search5turn28search17turn28search2

The most important negative result in this area is also clear. **Generation-first methods explode the candidate space very quickly.** The FOI papers say exactly that: standard-form tools that generate many hypotheses and then expect analysts to assess them one by one in ACH become impractical, so only a fraction can be evaluated while the rest are discarded. That failure mode is not incidental; it is one of the core reasons you cannot simply bolt “generation” onto vanilla ACH and expect it to scale. citeturn29view0turn29view1

My inference from the retrieved material is that ACH should be treated as, at most, a **thin front-end generator** via brainstorming and a **thick back-end evaluator** via comparative disproval. If your workflow needs systematic candidate-B generation, the literature supports pairing ACH with a more explicitly generative method such as morphological analysis, systematic design decomposition, C-K exploration, or contradiction-driven design methods. citeturn11search14turn29view0turn27view0turn27view5turn28search5

## How to make claim–evidence links mechanically checkable

For your third question, the most implementation-ready answer is not one representation but a **stack**. The stack I found in the literature has three layers: a **claim/argument structure**, a **provenance layer**, and a **validation layer**. The most directly relevant extra domain here is **assurance cases**, because that field already represents auditable claims supported by evidence and cares about missing support, context, and counter-evidence. The OMG SACM standard defines an assurance case as a set of **auditable claims, arguments, and evidence**, and the GSN community standard defines GSN as a graphical notation for explicitly documenting claims, evidence, and contextual information. citeturn34search1turn40view0

If you need a practical graph structure for “every claim in B must cite evidence,” **GSN/SACM is one of the best templates I found**. GSN separates goals or claims, strategies, contextual elements, and solutions or references to evidence. The standard even gives a bottom-up construction method: start from relevant evidence, ask what claim that evidence supports, create bottom-level “evidence assertion” goals, and then add higher goals. It also warns against prematurely “jumping” from a high-level goal straight to evidence without making the argumentative relationship understandable, and it explicitly requires consideration of **counter-evidence** and evidence integrity. All of that is directly aligned with your requirement that every modeled claim should carry explicit evidential grounding rather than a hand-wavy citation. citeturn40view2turn40view3

If you want a more general-purpose and machine-readable argument graph, **AIF** is the key bridge from argumentation theory to engineering. The AIF specification describes itself as an interlingua for argumentation formats and defines a graph with **information nodes** and **scheme nodes** for inference, conflict, and preference. That is useful if you need not only claim→evidence links, but also support, attack, and preference links between claims. The current OVA tool from ARG-tech builds on this family and explicitly supports collaborative graphical argument analysis with PDF annotation. citeturn37view1turn37view2

If you want something closer to Toulmin but actually enforceable, the **Argument Model Ontology** is especially important. AMO is an OWL ontology for the Toulmin model, and its published class constraints say that an argument has **exactly one claim** and at least **one evidence** and **one warrant**. That is the strongest direct answer I found to your “can we automatically check for orphan claims?” question. In other words, the ontology itself is already written in a form where missing required links can be flagged mechanically. It is not merely a diagramming convention. citeturn37view4

For provenance, the W3C **PROV-O** standard is the obvious backbone. PROV-O is a standard ontology for representing provenance information across systems, and the W3C **SHACL** standard is explicitly for validating RDF graphs against structural conditions. Nanopublications show how these pieces can be combined in practice: a nanopublication consists of an **assertion graph**, a **provenance graph for that assertion**, and **publication info**, and the guidelines include explicit well-formedness criteria such as exactly one assertion graph, exactly one provenance graph, exactly one publication-info graph, and the requirement that provenance triples reference the assertion. That is already very close to “every claim must hang on evidence and every evidence link must be inspectable.” citeturn14search0turn14search1turn38view0turn38view1

This stack also supports two different kinds of checking, which should be kept separate. The first is **structural validation**: does every claim node have at least one supported-by edge, does every evidence node have provenance, is every nanopublication well formed, are there orphaned claims or dangling evidence links? SHACL and ontological cardinality constraints are good at that. The second is **semantic support validation**: does the cited source text actually support the linked claim? That second problem is much harder, but it is no longer purely manual research. ProVe, for example, is a four-stage automated provenance-verification pipeline for knowledge graphs that extracts text from a cited source, verbalizes the claim, selects relevant passages, and then performs claim verification to decide whether the provenance actually supports the structured triple. citeturn14search1turn37view4turn38view0turn35view0

The literature also shows that once claims and provenance are represented structurally, additional reasoning becomes possible. Work on nanopublications demonstrates contradiction detection by reasoning over the **assertion and provenance graphs**, even when there are too few studies for classical meta-analysis. The Evidence Graph work likewise aims to represent supporting and challenging evidence for results and claims, extending provenance-style models with defeasible reasoning ideas. This matters for your workflow because it shows that “claim hangs on evidence” can be used not just for traceability, but for **inconsistency detection across claims**. citeturn38view2turn38view3turn13search3turn13search7

My implementation inference is that the simplest robust design for B is not a free-form document with footnotes. It is a graph in which each atomic claim is a first-class object, each supporting evidence item is a first-class object, each link is typed, provenance is explicit, and validation rules are codified. A minimal practical stack would be something like **GSN or AIF/AMO for the argument graph, PROV-O or nanopublication-style provenance for source lineage, and SHACL for orphan detection and integrity checks**, with optional provenance-verification models to flag unsupported links for human review. That inference is directly grounded in the standards and tools above, even though the exact stack choice is not prescribed by any single source. citeturn34search0turn34search1turn37view1turn37view4turn14search0turn14search1turn38view0turn35view0

## How GRADE actually maps evidence to recommendations and what transfers beyond medicine

For your fourth question, the key thing the GRADE literature says is that the mapping is **not** a single-axis function from “evidence strength” to “recommendation strength.” GRADE starts by rating the **certainty of evidence**. The handbook says this begins with study design and then considers five factors that can rate certainty down—risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision, and publication bias—and three that can rate certainty up, such as large effects and dose-response. It also says that when certainty differs across critical outcomes, the **lowest certainty among the critical outcomes** determines the overall certainty used for the recommendation. citeturn18view1

Recommendation strength is a different judgment. The handbook says the strength of a recommendation reflects how confident a panel is that desirable effects outweigh undesirable effects, or vice versa, across the intended population. It identifies **four key factors** for recommendation strength: balance of desirable and undesirable consequences, quality/certainty of evidence, values and preferences of those affected, and resource use. The broader EtD frameworks then enlarge that decision record with additional criteria such as whether the problem is a priority, equity impact, acceptability, and feasibility. The BMJ EtD paper emphasizes that these frameworks were built to make the move from evidence to recommendations transparent rather than implicit. citeturn18view0turn18view2turn19view0turn19view2turn19view5

That is why GRADE explicitly allows combinations that look odd if one expects a formula. The handbook states that **weak or conditional recommendations can be common even when confidence in effect estimates is high**, because non-evidential domains affect the final decision. It also states, more cautiously, that strong recommendations may sometimes be made despite low or very low certainty, while stressing that this should be unusual. So the real mapping rule is closer to “structured multi-criterion deliberation with explicit judgment recording” than to “certainty bucket X mechanically implies recommendation bucket Y.” citeturn18view0turn18view2turn17search0

The best evidence that this really is an engineering artifact and not just a theoretical rubric comes from the EtD testing paper. In 15 real guideline panels, the framework was described as helping structure a complex process in an explicit and transparent way, and as helping the formulation and strength assessment of recommendations. At the same time, the paper reports that some sections—especially **values and preferences** and the **balance between benefits and harms**—needed further development and clarification. In other words, even in medicine, the pain points are exactly the subjective and context-heavy fields that you would expect to become harder outside medicine. citeturn42view0

Can it transfer outside medicine? The retrieved evidence says **yes, but with adaptation and friction**. GRADE has already been generalized beyond individual clinical recommendations to **health systems**, **public health**, **coverage decisions**, **tests**, **adoption/adaptation/de novo guideline development**, and even **modeled evidence**. The public-health and health-system EtD paper says the same basic structure is used across different decision types. The ADOLOPMENT paper says the framework supports adoption, adaptation, and de novo recommendation development. The modeled-evidence paper shows GRADE being adapted to evidence from models rather than only direct trial data. A newer environmental and occupational health extension says GRADE now includes a specific EtD perspective for that domain. citeturn18view4turn24search15turn24search2turn24search6turn25search6

But the public-health challenge literature is equally clear that transfer is not frictionless. The GRADE Public Health Group’s concept article identifies five priority challenge areas: integrating diverse stakeholders, prioritizing health and non-health outcomes, interpreting outcomes and thresholds for decision-making, assessing certainty from diverse evidence sources including nonrandomized studies, and dealing with implications for decision makers including concerns about conditional recommendations. The same article notes that public-health decisions often involve competing policy goals, multiple sectors, lack of randomized studies, and stakeholders beyond health. That is already a partial answer to your “can this be moved outside medicine?” question: **it can, but only once you admit that the non-clinical context changes what counts as an outcome, whose values matter, and what evidence designs are available.** citeturn23view0

My implementation inference is that GRADE is most portable **as a decision-record schema**, not as a domain-independent scoring formula. If you transplant it to a non-medical setting, the durable part is the requirement to record, per candidate recommendation or conclusion, the evidence summary, certainty judgment, trade-off judgment, stakeholder/value judgment, resource judgment, and implementability/context judgments. The least portable part is the meaning of “certainty” and the conventions for adjudicating nonrandomized evidence, indirectness, or thresholds. The literature already shows this in the move from medicine to public health, environmental health, and modeled evidence. citeturn18view2turn19view5turn42view0turn23view0turn24search2turn25search6

## Operational methods for generating candidate outcomes from constraints and evidence

For your fifth question, the literature does contain several **procedural** methods that can generate candidate solutions or modeled outcomes from constraints and evidence. They differ mainly in the shape of the search space they assume.

**General Morphological Analysis** is the most directly applicable to discrete, structured requirement spaces. Ritchey describes it as a method for structuring a conceptual problem space and synthesizing a solution space. The operational flow is: define the key parameters of the problem, define discrete values for each parameter, generate the morphospace of combinations, run **cross-consistency assessment** on parameter-value pairs, and retain only internally consistent configurations. The FOI intelligence work then adds a useful twist: attach evidence to parameter values rather than to complete hypotheses, and propagate support or contradiction across all candidates containing that value. The main failure mode is obvious and well documented: the quality of the result depends heavily on choosing the right parameters and values, and the unconstrained combination count grows explosively. citeturn12search8turn27view3turn27view4turn29view0turn29view3

**Systematic engineering design in the Pahl–Beitz tradition** is a second strong candidate when B has a decomposable structure. The direct flow is: clarify the task, produce a requirements list that explicitly records requirements and constraints, abstract the essential problems, construct a function structure, search for candidate working principles for each subfunction, combine compatible principles into full working structures, and then evaluate concept variants. Pahl and Beitz explicitly state that the morphological matrix is particularly suitable for combining solution elements, and they also explicitly name the two main practical problems: ensuring compatibility among combined principles and selecting favorable combinations from a large combinatorial field. This is useful when A can be decomposed into subfunctions or subclaims that later need to be recombined into full candidate Bs. citeturn27view0turn27view2

**C-K theory** is best when the search space is not yet stable. The industrial-applications paper defines design as the co-expansion of concept and knowledge spaces, with operators moving from concept to knowledge, knowledge to concept, and internal expansions within each space. Operationally, that means one iterates between candidate concept generation and targeted knowledge search rather than trying to enumerate a closed solution space up front. This is a good fit when requirement A is structured but underdetermines the final realized form of B, so the workflow needs to progressively refine both the candidate outcome and the knowledge base that justifies it. The trade-off is that C-K is less “plug-and-play” than morphological analysis; it gives a disciplined exploration logic, but not a simple scoring matrix. citeturn27view5turn12search10

**TRIZ and ARIZ** are useful when the core difficulty is contradiction rather than decomposition. The ARIZ material says to formulate a **mini-problem**, define the **conflicting elements**, and then proceed through a structured algorithm. More common TRIZ practice uses the contradiction matrix: choose what parameter you want to improve, identify what parameter worsens, and use the matrix to retrieve candidate inventive principles. The literature also cautions that ARIZ is difficult to follow and cognitively demanding, and that the principles do not return finished solutions; they point the user toward classes of solution moves. So TRIZ is procedural, but it is better seen as a contradiction-navigation engine than as a complete evidence-to-outcome method. citeturn28search5turn28search17turn28search2

**Bayesian-network formalization and related probabilistic models** are more evaluative than generative, but they become valuable immediately after candidate generation. The Bayesian extensions to ACH show why: they can encode priors, conditional dependence, context variables, intermediate explanatory variables, and then support sensitivity analysis and information-gathering priorities. The critique of plain ACH is that it handles dependencies poorly, especially for rumors, deception, or contextual interaction among hypotheses and evidence. So if you first generate candidates with a discrete or design method, a Bayesian layer can then help test which candidate outcome models are fragile, which assumptions dominate the answer, and where new evidence would reduce uncertainty most efficiently. citeturn30view4turn30view5

The most important cross-method finding is negative: **no single method here solves both generation and evidence-auditable justification well**. Morphological analysis and systematic design are good at candidate generation and pruning. ACH and Bayesian approaches are better at comparative evaluation. GSN/AIF/SACM and provenance standards are better at preserving the support structure once a candidate is selected. That division of labor is not a weakness of the literature; it is arguably the main engineering lesson of the literature. citeturn29view0turn27view0turn30view5turn34search0turn37view1turn38view0
